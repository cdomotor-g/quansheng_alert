Build a programming cable

The pinout of the Quansheng UV-K5’s two-pin socket, what to build a cable from, and — because published pinouts disagree with each other — how to check your wiring with a multimeter before you plug it into the radio.

Read this first

A ready-made cable costs about $10 and takes five minutes to buy. Building one is worth it if you have the parts on the bench tonight, or you want a cable that also breaks out the serial line for logging the ALERT readings the firmware sends. It is not worth it to save the money.

The risk is real but small. The radio’s serial pins go straight to a 3.3 V microcontroller. Connecting a 5 V adapter’s transmit line to them, or shorting a signal to the battery rail, can damage that chip — and unlike the firmware, that is not something you can put back. Use a 3.3 V adapter, keep the series resistor, and do the measurement checks below before the first connection.

What the socket is

The UV-K5 uses the Kenwood “K1” two-pin arrangement, the same as a Baofeng UV-5R: a 3.5 mm jack and a 2.5 mm jack side by side under the rubber flap, so a single moulded plug engages both at once. Ordinary audio plugs work; you just have to hold them at the right spacing, which is what the moulded shell of a bought cable does for you.

The pinout

Only three connections matter: ground, the line the radio talks on, and the line it listens on. Everything else in the socket — speaker, microphone, battery voltage — is left alone.

Read the caveat before you cut anything. The table below is traced from the community’s reverse-engineered KiCad schematic of the UV-K5 PCB (sheet USB2SERIAL.kicad_sch, titled “Kenwood Programming Cable”), which is the best primary source there is. But that sheet labels its two plug symbols the opposite way round from the universal Kenwood K1 convention — it puts the speaker on the 2.5 mm plug and the microphone on the 3.5 mm one, where every radio of this family has speaker on the 3.5 mm. Other published pinouts disagree again on which conductor carries ground. So: the signals below are solid and agreed by every source; which physical conductor each lands on is worth measuring on your own radio, which takes two minutes and settles it for the radio in your hand.

V+ (battery) Present on one sleeve. Leave it unconnected. nothing measure it
Speaker, MIC+ Audio. Not used for programming. nothing schematic

confirmed = the reverse-engineered schematic and the published community pinouts agree. measure it = sources disagree, so find it on your own radio with the procedure below. Either way the measurement takes two minutes and is the last word.

One consequence of the shared line worth understanding. The radio’s transmit line doubles as MIC−/PTT — the line the radio watches to decide whether you are pressing the push-to-talk button. Pulling it to ground can therefore key the transmitter. That is why the adapter’s RX — an input, which never drives anything — is the pin that goes there, and why the series resistor is on the other line. Get those two the wrong way round without the resistor and you can have the adapter fighting the radio’s output, and the radio transmitting when you did not ask it to.

At the microcontroller end these are pins PA07 (UART1 TX) and PA08 (UART1 RX) of the DP32G030, at 38400 baud, 8N1. That much is certain: it matches both the wiki’s pin assignment and this firmware’s own board.c, which configures exactly those two pins for UART1.

Check the wiring before you plug it in

Published pinouts for these radios disagree with one another, and Quansheng have changed details between production runs. Two minutes of measuring beats trusting any diagram, including this one.

1. Find ground, with the radio switched off

  1. Take the battery off the radio.
  2. Set the meter to continuity. Put one probe on the metal shell of the antenna socket — that is chassis ground.
  3. Touch the other probe to each contact of each jack in turn. The contact that beeps is ground. Write down which one it is; it should match the table above.

2. Find the radio’s transmit line, with the radio talking

This is the neat part, and it needs no special equipment. In bootloader mode the radio chatters continuously by itself — it announces “I am in flash mode” about twice a second whether or not anything is listening. So it drives its transmit line without your having to send it anything.

  1. Battery back on. Switch the radio off.
  2. Hold PTT and switch it on while still holding. The torch LED glows white and the screen stays blank.
  3. Meter on DC volts, black probe on the ground contact you found in step 1.
  4. Probe the other contacts. The radio’s transmit line sits at about 3.3 V and twitches downward twice a second as it sends. On a cheap meter that shows as a reading that will not settle — flickering somewhere between roughly 3.0 and 3.3 V rather than sitting still.
  5. A contact sitting rock steady at 3.3 V or at 0 V is not it. A contact at battery voltage (7–8 V) is a power pin — never connect the adapter to that one.

If you own a USB logic analyser or a scope, the same trick gives you the whole answer at once: the bootloader’s chatter is 38400 baud 8N1, and each burst starts with the bytes AB CD. Seeing those two bytes decode confirms the line, the polarity and the baud rate in one shot.

3. Sanity-check the adapter before it meets the radio

  1. With the adapter plugged into the computer and nothing else connected, measure its transmit pin against its ground: 3.3 V, not 5 V. If it reads 5 V, find the voltage jumper, or use a different adapter.
  2. Short the adapter’s transmit and receive pins together and open any serial terminal at 38400 baud: what you type should come back. That proves the adapter and its driver work before the radio is anywhere near it.

Wiring it up

Three wires. Keep the run short and twist them loosely together.

  USB-TTL adapter                              radio
  (set to 3.3 V)

     GND  ─────────────────────────────────►  ground contact
     RX   ─────────────────────────────────►  radio TX  (the MIC-/PTT contact)
     TX   ────────[ 1 kΩ ]──────────────────►  radio RX

     5V / VCC   ── not connected
                                              speaker, MIC+, V+  ── not connected
  1. Set the adapter to 3.3 V and confirm it with the meter before anything else. This is the step that protects the radio.
  2. Solder the 1 kΩ resistor in series with the adapter’s TX line. It costs nothing and turns a mis-wire from “damaged radio” into “does not work”.
  3. Wire ground first, then the two data lines per the table above.
  4. Leave every other contact unconnected. In particular do not connect the adapter’s 5 V pin to anything.
  5. Insulate each joint before the next, and heatshrink the lot. The two plugs sit close together and a whisker of solder between them is easy to create and hard to see.

Remember that the adapter’s TX goes to the radio’s RX and vice versa. Wiring TX to TX is the classic mistake and the reason the troubleshooting list below starts with “swap them”.

Test it

  1. Plug the cable into the radio and the computer. Switch the radio on normally.
  2. Open the installer, press Connect and pick the adapter.
  3. Open Toolbox → What is my radio running? and press Ask the radio. If it answers with a version such as 2.01.26, the cable is good in both directions — the radio received a command and its reply got back.

That check is deliberately harmless: it only asks the radio to identify itself and writes nothing. Do it before trusting the cable with a firmware install.

If it does not answer

  • Nothing appears in the browser’s port list: the adapter’s driver is missing, not a wiring fault. Install the CH340 driver (or the one for your chip) and re-plug it.
  • Port opens, radio silent: transmit and receive are the usual culprits — swap them at the adapter end. With the 1 kΩ resistor in place this is safe to try, and it is the single most common mistake.
  • Still silent: check the plugs are fully home. These jacks are stiff, and a plug that is a millimetre out contacts the wrong ring.
  • Garbage instead of answers: baud rate. The radio is 38400 8N1 and is not negotiable.
  • Recheck ground last — if ground is wrong nothing works at all, so it is rarely the survivor of the earlier tests.

What else the cable is good for

Once built, the same cable carries the ALERT receiver’s output. With the firmware running, every decoded reading is written to the serial port as a line of CSV:

ALERT,6129,1599,ABF,-98,LOUDOUN BR

— address, value, format, signal strength in dBm, and the station name. Point any serial terminal or logging script at the port at 38400 baud to record a session.
