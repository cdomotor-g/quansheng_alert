Build a programming cable

The pinout of the Quansheng UV-K5’s two-pin socket, what to build a cable from, and — because published pinouts disagree with each other — how to check your wiring with a multimeter before you plug it into the radio.

Read this first

A ready-made cable costs about $10 and takes five minutes to buy. Building one is worth it if you have the parts on the bench tonight, or you want a cable that also breaks out the serial line for logging the ALERT readings the firmware sends. It is not worth it to save the money.

The risk is real but small. The radio’s serial pins go straight to a 3.3 V microcontroller. Connecting a 5 V adapter’s transmit line to them, or shorting a signal to the battery rail, can damage that chip — and unlike the firmware, that is not something you can put back. Use a 3.3 V adapter, keep the series resistor, and do the measurement checks below before the first connection.

What the socket is

The UV-K5 uses the Kenwood “K1” two-pin arrangement, the same as a Baofeng UV-5R: a 3.5 mm jack and a 2.5 mm jack side by side under the rubber flap, so a single moulded plug engages both at once. Ordinary audio plugs work; you just have to hold them at the right spacing, which is what the moulded shell of a bought cable does for you.

The pinout

Only three connections matter: ground, the line the radio listens on, and the line it talks on. Everything else in the socket — speaker, microphone, battery voltage — is left alone. Both plugs are three-conductor (TRS), not two-conductor.

Sources: the CHIRP cable guide, Miklor’s pinout table, the UV-K5 wiki’s reverse-engineered KiCad schematic, and the Baofeng UV-5R factory schematic, whose net on that contact is named RXD/PTT — the same K1 wiring.

Why the radio’s receive line is also its PTT line, and what that means for you. Pressing push-to-talk shorts that contact to ground. A line a switch is allowed to short to ground has to be an input — so it is the radio’s UART receive pin, and a long low on it reads as “PTT pressed”. Two consequences: the adapter’s TX is what belongs there, and your adapter must idle high. A normal 3.3 V TTL adapter does. An RS-232 level converter idles low and will hold the radio in transmit the moment you plug it in — never wire one of those straight to this socket.

PCB v1.6 hazard. On that board revision the 2.5 mm tip carries about 3.6–3.8 V DC (it does not on v1.4). Shorting it while fishing around with a probe or a half-inserted plug is a genuine way to damage something. Leave it alone, and seat the plugs fully.

These two lines are separate and dedicated — this is not a half-duplex single-wire interface. Do not join TX and RX through a resistor or diode; that trick belongs to other radios and breaks this one.

At the microcontroller they are pins PA07 (UART1 TX) and PA08 (UART1 RX) of the DP32G030, 38400 baud, 8N1, non-inverted, idle high. The baud rate is not negotiable — the radio accepts no other. That much agrees with the wiki’s pin assignment and with this firmware’s own board.c.

Where the published sources disagree, and how it was settled

Worth knowing if you find a diagram that contradicts the table above.

  • Miklor contradicts itself on its own page: the table and the build paragraph swap TX and RX. Miklor explains why — “some boards have the TxD and RxD pin labels reversed” — so that paragraph is describing particular adapter silkscreens, not signal direction. Trust the direction, not the silkscreen.
  • The KiCad schematic names its nets from the adapter’s point of view (“USB2SER RX”), which reads backwards if you take it as the radio’s. Tracing that sheet’s wires by hand — which is how the first version of this page was written — gives TX and RX the wrong way round for exactly that reason. The PTT argument above is what settles it, because it does not depend on anyone’s labelling.
  • Plug order is reported inconsistently by vendors. Identify the two plugs by diameter, never by which is on top. Centre-to-centre spacing is quoted between 11 and 12 mm — measure your radio rather than trusting a number.
  • “5 V TTL” appears in some guides. That is a generic Kenwood statement. The DP32G030 runs at 3.3 V and its manual does not publish whether PA7/PA8 are among the 5 V-tolerant pins, so treat them as 3.3 V.

Check the wiring before you plug it in

Published pinouts for these radios disagree with one another, and Quansheng have changed details between production runs. Two minutes of measuring beats trusting any diagram, including this one.

1. Find ground, with the radio switched off

  1. Take the battery off the radio.
  2. Set the meter to continuity. Put one probe on the metal shell of the antenna socket — that is chassis ground.
  3. Touch the other probe to each contact of each jack in turn. The contact that beeps is ground. Write down which one it is; it should match the table above.

2. Find the radio’s transmit line, with the radio talking

This is the neat part, and it needs no special equipment. In bootloader mode the radio chatters continuously by itself — it announces “I am in flash mode” about twice a second whether or not anything is listening. So it drives its transmit line without your having to send it anything.

  1. Battery back on. Switch the radio off.
  2. Hold PTT and switch it on while still holding. The torch LED glows white and the screen stays blank.
  3. Meter on DC volts, black probe on the ground contact you found in step 1.
  4. Probe the other contacts. The radio’s transmit line — the 2.5 mm ring, per the table — sits at about 3.3 V and twitches downward twice a second as it sends. On a cheap meter that shows as a reading that will not settle: flickering somewhere between roughly 3.0 and 3.3 V rather than sitting still.
  5. Now switch the radio off and back on normally and check the 3.5 mm sleeve: it should sit steady at about 3.3 V, because it is an input idling high. If it reads near 0 V, stop — you have the wrong contact, and connecting the adapter’s transmit line to it will not work and may key the radio.
  6. A contact sitting rock steady at 3.3 V or at 0 V is not it. A contact at battery voltage (7–8 V) is a power pin — never connect the adapter to that one.

If you own a USB logic analyser or a scope, the same trick gives you the whole answer at once: the bootloader’s chatter is 38400 baud 8N1, and each burst starts with the bytes AB CD. Seeing those two bytes decode confirms the line, the polarity and the baud rate in one shot.

3. Sanity-check the adapter before it meets the radio

  1. With the adapter plugged into the computer and nothing else connected, measure its transmit pin against its ground: 3.3 V, not 5 V. If it reads 5 V, find the voltage jumper, or use a different adapter.
  2. Short the adapter’s transmit and receive pins together and open any serial terminal at 38400 baud: what you type should come back. That proves the adapter and its driver work before the radio is anywhere near it.

Wiring it up

Three wires. Keep the run short and twist them loosely together.

  USB-TTL adapter                              radio
  (set to 3.3 V)

     GND  ─────────────────────────────────►  2.5 mm  sleeve   ground
     RX   ─────────────────────────────────►  2.5 mm  ring     radio TX
     TX   ────────[ 1 kΩ ]──────────────────►  3.5 mm  sleeve   radio RX / PTT

     5V / VCC   ── not connected
                                              2.5 mm tip (speaker, DC on v1.6) ── not connected
                                              3.5 mm tip (+V), 3.5 mm ring (MIC+) ── not connected
  1. Set the adapter to 3.3 V and confirm it with the meter before anything else. This is the step that protects the radio.
  2. Solder the 1 kΩ resistor in series with the adapter’s TX line. It costs nothing and turns a mis-wire from “damaged radio” into “does not work”.
  3. Wire ground first, then the two data lines per the table above.
  4. If you only own a 5 V adapter, divide its TX down: 1 kΩ in series and 2.2 kΩ from the plug end to ground gives about 3.4 V. The radio’s own 3.3 V output drives a 5 V adapter’s RX without help.
  5. Leave every other contact unconnected. In particular do not connect the adapter’s 5 V pin to anything.
  6. Insulate each joint before the next, and heatshrink the lot. The two plugs sit close together and a whisker of solder between them is easy to create and hard to see.

Remember that the adapter’s TX goes to the radio’s RX and vice versa. Wiring TX to TX is the classic mistake and the reason the troubleshooting list below starts with “swap them”.

Test it

  1. Plug the cable into the radio and the computer. Switch the radio on normally.
  2. Open the installer, press Connect and pick the adapter.
  3. Open Toolbox → What is my radio running? and press Ask the radio. If it answers with a version such as 2.01.26, the cable is good in both directions — the radio received a command and its reply got back.

That check is deliberately harmless: it only asks the radio to identify itself and writes nothing. Do it before trusting the cable with a firmware install.

If it does not answer

  • Nothing appears in the browser’s port list: the adapter’s driver is missing, not a wiring fault. Install the CH340 driver (or the one for your chip) and re-plug it.
  • Port opens, radio silent: transmit and receive are the usual culprits — swap them at the adapter end. With the 1 kΩ resistor in place this is safe to try, and it is the single most common mistake.
  • Still silent: check the plugs are fully home. These jacks are stiff, and a plug that is a millimetre out contacts the wrong ring.
  • Garbage in a terminal: not, on its own, a baud-rate problem. The radio is 38400 8N1 and that is not negotiable — but its packets are binary and scrambled, so even at the right rate a terminal shows nonsense, in bootloader mode a burst of it about twice a second. Switch the terminal to a hex view: each burst should begin AB CD and end DC BA. If it does, the line is fine and the installer’s problem is on the computer side (usually another program holding the port). If it does not, let the diagnostic script try every rate and check the checksums.
  • Recheck ground last — if ground is wrong nothing works at all, so it is rarely the survivor of the earlier tests.

Letting a script do the fault-finding

tools/k5diag.py in the repository runs the checks above from the command line and says which side is at fault. It needs Python 3 and pip install pyserial, and it never writes to the radio — the only thing it ever sends is the same harmless “what are you running?” question as the Toolbox.

python tools/k5diag.py --port COM3            # every check, with a verdict
python tools/k5diag.py --port COM3 listen     # radio in bootloader mode: are beacons framing at 38400?
python tools/k5diag.py --port COM3 scan       # same, at every plausible baud rate
python tools/k5diag.py --port COM3 hello      # radio on normally: does it answer with its version?
python tools/k5diag.py --port COM3 loopback   # adapter TX shorted to RX: does the adapter work at all?
python tools/k5diag.py ports                  # which port is the cable?

With the radio in bootloader mode, listen is the decisive test: it counts the beacons that pass their checksum and reads the bootloader’s version out of them. If it reports beacons, baud rate, polarity and the radio’s transmit line are all proven at once. The installer’s Toolbox → Check the cable runs the same sequence in the browser, and Test the adapter alone is the loopback.

What else the cable is good for

Once built, the same cable carries the ALERT receiver’s output. With the firmware running, every decoded reading is written to the serial port as a line of CSV:

ALERT,6129,1599,ABF,-98,LOUDOUN BR

— address, value, format, signal strength in dBm, and the station name. Point any serial terminal or logging script at the port at 38400 baud to record a session.
